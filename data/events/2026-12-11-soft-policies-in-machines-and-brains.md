## Abstract

<!-- Use HTML so Markdown leaves the speaker's apostrophe in "agent's" intact. -->
<p>A soft policy is a decision-making rule that intentionally leaves a degree of randomness in action selection. It is popular in the RL community for training robustness, especially when encountering noisy and uncertain environments. In our preprint, we build a connection between machines and biological brains regarding soft policies. We ask whether the origins of randomness in biological decision-making similarly arise due to soft policies. From a dynamic-programming analysis of optimality, we prove that soft policies with randomness, rather than deterministic policies, are optimal when considering the cost of control from information-thermodynamic principles by Landauer. From the analysis of macaque data, we show that the origins of randomness—between soft policies and the agent's noisy belief before the action policies—lead to distinctive behavioral patterns. The macaques show both. This theoretical work results in a convergence with the random utility theory by McFadden. And it may update the image of the decision boundary that has lasted for decades in decision-making theory from hard lines to soft stopping zones.</p>

## About the speaker

Bo Shen recently joined the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, in Beijing as a Principal Investigator. Before that, he conducted postdoctoral research with Dr. Paul Glimcher at the NYU School of Medicine. With an interdisciplinary academic background, he earned his bachelor’s degree in physics from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and his Ph.D. in Cognitive Psychology from Peking University. His research focuses on understanding the neural and computational mechanisms of decision-making. He combines human neuroscience approaches, including functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), and choice trajectory tracking (mouse-tracking), with computational modeling of neural circuits, including the development of a disinhibition-based circuit model of decision-making. Looking ahead, his research aims to investigate the neural and computational principles underlying decision-making in dynamic environments.
